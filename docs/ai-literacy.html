Foundational AI Literacy for Research Administrators

A practical foundation in AI for research administration: how it works, what it can do, where it falls short, and how to use it responsibly.

Nathan Layman · AI4RA / University of Idaho

What "literacy" has always meant

"Reading the world always precedes reading the word, and reading the word implies continually reading the world."

Paulo Freire, Literacy: Reading the Word and the World (1987).

"The ability to understand, evaluate, use and engage with written texts to participate in society, to achieve one's goals, and to develop one's knowledge and potential."

OECD PIAAC Literacy Framework.

Digital literacy

UNESCO defines digital literacy with seven verbs.

"The ability to access, manage, understand, integrate, communicate, evaluate and create information safely and appropriately … to build resilience in the face of disinformation."

UNESCO's definition of digital literacy. Same skills as literacy. New class of tool.

AI literacy

The same verbs, plus one.

"AI literacy equips learners to understand how AI systems work, critically evaluate their outputs, and use them ethically and creatively."

OECD & European Commission, Empowering Learners for the Age of AI (2025). Long & Magerko (ACM CHI 2020) add the reframe: not just evaluate AI, but collaborate with it.

Four reasons research administrators should be AI-literate.

1. To be empowered

Use AI to create, draft, and integrate. Make the tools work for you.

2. To know what to trust

Read output critically. Catch hallucinations before they become decisions.

3. To be safe

Right tool. Right data. Within institutional rules.

4. To communicate

Understand what AI is. Talk about it clearly with faculty and sponsors.

The frame

Research administration upholds the contract between sponsors and researchers.

These contracts are both legal and social, and research administrators hold all of them at the same time.

The primary contract

Sponsor ↔ Researcher

Upheld by research administration.

Explicit

  • 2 CFR 200 and agency T&Cs
  • IRB / IACUC / IBC approvals
  • Subrecipient agreements
  • F&A rate agreement
  • FCOI disclosures
  • Effort commitments
  • Data-sharing plans
  • Cost share

Implicit

  • Accuracy of what is submitted
  • Timeliness of reporting
  • Responsible stewardship of federal dollars
  • Scientific integrity
  • Transparency with reviewers
  • The institution’s public license

AI literacy means understanding how AI touches the contracts you uphold, because an AI cannot be held to any of them and the responsibility stays with you.

For research administrators, understanding and evaluation come before prompting.

  1. Understand · a working mental model of what modern AI is (and isn't).
  2. Evaluate · read output critically; verify before forwarding.
  3. Manage · know what data can go where.
  4. Communicate · write prompts that work; explain AI clearly.
  5. Access · choose the right tool for the job.
  6. Create · draft, summarize, outline; AI as first-pass collaborator.
  7. Integrate · fit AI into workflows without breaking them.

One more thing

Literacy is a spectrum.

You don't need to train a model, tune weights, or debug a transformer. That's someone else's job.

Enough of a mental model to hit the four reasons. Effective use, not rabbit-hole depth.

Module 1 · Working definition

AI is any tool where we cannot say exactly how it arrived at the answer.

The output can be repeatable. The reasoning cannot be narrated. Inferred is about how, not what.

We approve the workflow, not the individual output. Validation, human review, and audit trails carry the assurance load.

Module 1 · A mental model

Imagine 1,000 broadly-informed strangers.

An LLM behaves like a crowd surveying itself and reporting the consensus.

Strong at

Consensus knowledge. Common sense. Drafting. Summarizing.

Weak at

Novel ideas. Contested claims. Specialist material. Anything recent.

Asking the same question twice is not verification. It is polling the same crowd twice.

Module 1 · Task fit

AI, or data science? Match the task.

Data science

Reproducible. Precise. Auditable.

Reach for it first when the answer must be reproducible from structured data.

AI

Flexible. Handles unstructured text. Needs validation.

Reach for it when the input is language, or the task is pattern recognition.

Module 1 · Choosing the right tools

The AI appropriateness continuum.

Process Critical Human-in-the-Loop Output-Driven
HOW it was done matters most WHAT was produced matters most

Limited AI involvement

Use auditable, reproducible tools: scripts, queries, spreadsheets with visible logic.

  • Financial audits
  • Effort certification & sign-off
  • Conflict-of-interest determinations
  • IRB/IACUC protocol reviews
  • Research misconduct investigations

AI can assist

AI does the prep. A qualified person reviews, validates, and signs off.

  • Pre-award budget review for allowability
  • Compliance checks against sponsor terms
  • Subaward risk assessments
  • Cost transfer justification review
  • Closeout checklist preparation

AI can lead

AI leads, but measure accuracy. Spot-check, track errors, add review back in if quality slips.

  • Drafting facilities/equipment descriptions
  • Formatting budgets to sponsor templates
  • Summarizing institutional policies
  • Generating biosketches from CVs
  • Routing form auto-population

Module 1 · Applying the framework

Two questions to pick the right tools.

1

If someone asked how this task was completed, would the answer matter?

2

Could a colleague re-run it and get the same result?

Two yeses point to process critical work, mixed answers to human-in-the-loop, and situations where a similar result is acceptable to output-driven.

Module 1 · Human-in-the-loop, done right

Outputs are validated. Workflows are approved.

Workflows are approved by validating their outputs: the validation provides the evidence on which a person shoulders the responsibility to cover what the workflow produces.

Step 1 · per instance

Validate outputs

Spot-check outputs, sample them, and measure error rates to gather evidence that the workflow is behaving as expected.

  • Is this specific output accurate?
  • Does it meet the requirement?
  • What is the error rate over time?
→
Step 2 · a person signs off

Approve the workflow

Based on that evidence, a person shoulders responsibility for what the workflow produces.

  • Who owns this workflow?
  • Where are the checkpoints and audit trail?
  • What triggers re-approval?

“A computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer must never make a management decision.”

IBM training manual, 1979

This is why AI will not replace research administrators: the job is approval, approval requires accountability, and only a person can carry accountability.

Module 4 · Data classification drives tool choice

Which tool for which data?

ChatGPT free tier, Gemini free, Claude free.

  • Fast, accessible, no procurement
  • Inputs may train the model
  • Not for PII, PHI, FERPA, or export-controlled data
Good for: public FOAs, summarizing published papers, drafting boilerplate.

Institutional Copilot, private Claude/Gemini via enterprise agreement, Vandalizer.

  • Inputs stay within your institution's data boundary
  • Approved for defined sensitivity levels (varies by contract)
  • Confirm your BAA / data-processing terms before use
Good for: internal proposal review, sponsor correspondence drafts, budget analysis.

SQL queries, structured spreadsheets, purpose-built scripts.

  • Reproducible. Auditable. Deterministic.
  • Required when regulators need to see the computation
  • Rigid: needs clean structured data going in
Good for: financial audits, effort certification, compliance reporting.

Session outcomes

Learning objectives.

  1. Explain foundational AI concepts and terminology relevant to research administration.
  2. Identify tasks and workflows in research administration where AI and automation can add value.
  3. Select appropriate AI models and break complex tasks into manageable components across different AI tools and platforms.
  4. Design effective and accurate AI prompts for specific administrative tasks.
  5. Evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, reliability, and appropriateness, and apply iterative refinement strategies broadly across AI systems.
  6. Be prepared to discuss and address ethical, transparency, and governance considerations in AI-assisted workflows.
