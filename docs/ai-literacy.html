What "literacy" has always meant

"The ability to understand, evaluate, use and engage with written texts to participate in society, to achieve one's goals, and to develop one's knowledge and potential."

OECD PIAAC Literacy Framework.

Digital literacy

UNESCO defines digital literacy with seven verbs.

"The ability to access, manage, understand, integrate, communicate, evaluate and create information safely and appropriately … to build resilience in the face of disinformation."

UNESCO's definition of digital literacy. Same skills as literacy. New class of tool.

AI literacy

The same verbs, plus one.

"AI literacy equips learners to understand how AI systems work, critically evaluate their outputs, and use them ethically and creatively."

OECD & European Commission (2025)

A working definition

AI literacy is knowledge that makes you faster at learning any new AI tool.

Restated: literacy is knowledge that is portable. If it survives switching to a different platform, a newer model, or a different kind of AI, it is literacy. If it does not, it is usage training, and it ages quickly.

Across platforms

What you know about Claude should transfer to ChatGPT, Gemini, and whatever comes next.

Across time

The specific tool changes every few months. The concepts behind it do not.

Across modalities

Text, image, audio, agents. Deeper literacy carries between them.

The practical test: if I switched to a different AI tool tomorrow, would I still need this knowledge? Yes means literacy. No means usage.

What falls inside

Eight kinds of knowledge that make you AI-literate.

  1. Mechanism. How the tools produce their output. Tokens, probability, training data cutoffs.
  2. Boundary. What AI can and cannot do. Strong at consensus, weak at specialist material and anything recent.
  3. Evaluation. Reading output critically. Recognizing hallucination patterns before they propagate.
  4. Data. What happens to your prompts. Retention, training, and where a given kind of data is allowed to go.
  5. Assurance. Designing workflows that use AI responsibly. Validation, approval, audit trails.
  6. Ethical. Recognizing when AI raises an ethical question rather than a technical one.
  7. Communication. Explaining AI accurately, without overpromising, to a PI, an auditor, or an IT lead.
  8. Contextual. How AI intersects with the contracts, policies, and stakeholders in your institution.

The rest of this session walks through these, in roughly this order.

Before we go further

Being literate about AI is not the same as knowing how to use AI.

Anyone can learn to type into a chatbot and get an answer. That is usage. Understanding why the answer is what it is, when to trust it, and where it will fail is literacy, and it is a different skill.

Usage

Writing prompts, picking a tool, getting the workflow done. Most of your office already does this at some level, whether or not it is sanctioned.

Literacy

Knowing how the tool works, why the output looks confident when it is wrong, and when to reach for something else. This is the skill that makes usage safe.

The rest of this session is about literacy. Usage is what you do with the literacy afterward.

Module 1 · Working definition

AI is any tool where we cannot say exactly how it arrived at the answer.

The output can be repeatable. The reasoning cannot be narrated. Inferred is about how, not what.

We approve the workflow, not the individual output. Validation, human review, and audit trails carry the assurance load.

Module 1 · A mental model

Imagine 1,000 broadly-informed strangers.

An LLM behaves like a crowd surveying itself and reporting the consensus.

Strong at

Consensus knowledge. Common sense. Drafting. Summarizing.

Weak at

Novel ideas. Contested claims. Specialist material. Anything recent.

Asking the same question twice is not verification. It is polling the same crowd twice.

Recognizing when the crowd is guessing is the literacy skill. Regulatory citations, specialist regs, and anything recent are exactly the places to check.

Module 1 · Task fit

Knowing which category a task belongs to is a literacy skill of its own.

Data science

Reproducible. Precise. Auditable.

Reach for it first when the answer must be reproducible from structured data.

AI

Flexible. Handles unstructured text. Needs validation.

Reach for it when the input is language, or the task is pattern recognition.

Module 1 · Choosing the right tools

The AI appropriateness continuum.

Process Critical Human-in-the-Loop Output-Driven
HOW it was done matters most WHAT was produced matters most

Limited AI involvement

Use auditable, reproducible tools: scripts, queries, spreadsheets with visible logic.

  • Financial audits
  • Effort certification & sign-off
  • Conflict-of-interest determinations
  • IRB/IACUC protocol reviews
  • Research misconduct investigations

AI can assist

AI does the prep. A qualified person reviews, validates, and signs off.

  • Pre-award budget review for allowability
  • Compliance checks against sponsor terms
  • Subaward risk assessments
  • Cost transfer justification review
  • Closeout checklist preparation

AI can lead

AI leads, but measure accuracy. Spot-check, track errors, add review back in if quality slips.

  • Drafting facilities/equipment descriptions
  • Formatting budgets to sponsor templates
  • Summarizing institutional policies
  • Generating biosketches from CVs
  • Routing form auto-population

Module 1 · Applying the framework

Two questions to pick the right tools.

1

If someone asked how this task was completed, would the answer matter?

2

Could a colleague re-run it and get the same result?

Two yeses point to process critical work, mixed answers to human-in-the-loop, and situations where a similar result is acceptable to output-driven.

Module 1 · Human-in-the-loop, done right

Assurance operates at two levels.

Output-level and workflow-level assurance are complementary, and skipping either one breaks the chain. Higher-stakes work leans more on assuring individual outputs, while lower-stakes work leans more on assuring the workflow that produces them.

Output-level · per instance

Assure each output

Depending on stakes, each output is either signed off individually or checked through a defensible sample.

  • High stakes: full sign-off on every one (IRB protocols, budget revisions, sponsor invoices).
  • Lower stakes: sampled validation with tracked error rates (reconciliations, closeout audits).
Workflow-level · upfront and ongoing

Assure the workflow

A named person owns the process itself, designs it to catch errors, and revisits the approval when it changes.

  • Who owns this workflow and what are they on the hook for?
  • Where are the checkpoints, and is there an audit trail?
  • What triggers a re-approval of the process?

“A computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer must never make a management decision.”

IBM training manual, 1979

This is why AI will not replace research administrators: both levels of assurance ultimately resolve to a person shouldering responsibility, which is something only a person can do.

Module 2 · So you can recognize when an AI decision raises an ethical question

Ethical and responsible AI use.

  • Four guiding questions. Vulnerability, harm, benefit, reversibility.
  • Two methodologies. Argument by analogy. Reflective equilibrium.
  • Three collective structures. Commons, prisoner's dilemma, stag hunt.
  • Bias, privacy, disclosure. FERPA, HIPAA, GDPR. Sponsor guidance.

Module 2 · Why it matters here

You sit at the center of a network of dependencies.

Every AI decision you make either honors or strains the trust the parties around you have placed in your office.

Research administration

Downstream · who depends on you

Principal investigators

Depend on you for accurate budgets, correct reading of awards, and compliance flags before they become incidents.

Your institution

Depends on you to safeguard data, maintain sponsor relationships, and act in ways the institution can stand behind.

Sponsors

Depend on the integrity of the documents you produce and the accuracy of what you submit on the institution’s behalf.

The public

Depends on responsible stewardship of federal dollars and the social license that funds research at your institution.

Upstream · whom you depend on

AI vendors

Shape what the tools can do, how they handle your prompts, and what changes without notice.

Institutional IT and security

Set which tools are sanctioned, where data can go, and what happens after an incident.

Training data and its subjects

The people, papers, and prior work behind every model output. Their consent and context shape yours.

Ethics in this setting is not abstract. It is deciding who bears the risk of an AI choice you make.

Module 2 · A decision framework

Four questions to ask before adopting AI in any workflow.

1

Who is made vulnerable?

A PI whose preliminary data is exposed. A subject whose consent did not cover this use. A junior staff member whose role is quietly reshaped.

2

Who benefits and who is harmed?

You may save thirty minutes on a boilerplate draft while a colleague loses the reps that would have made them competent at it.

3

Is the benefit worth the cost?

Weigh the time saved against the risk of a plausible but wrong regulatory citation reaching a sponsor.

4

Is it reversible?

A draft on your screen is easy to revise. A hallucinated statistic already in a submitted proposal is not.

Module 2 · A common trap

“Everyone uses it” is not the same as “everyone should.”

A statement about what people do is descriptive. A statement about what people ought to do is normative. Sliding from the first to the second, without the reasoning that connects them, is the naturalistic fallacy.

The most common form in an RA office: “Everyone in our office pastes drafts into ChatGPT already, so it must be fine.” That is a description of behavior. It is not, by itself, an argument that the behavior is permissible.

Module 2 · Practical posture

When you use AI on someone else’s work, tell them.

To the PI

Anything that touches their proposal, biosketch, budget narrative, or communication going out under their name.

To the sponsor

Where sponsor terms require it. NIH prohibits AI in peer review. Many journals require an AI-use statement. Check the specific solicitation.

To your team

Process changes, shared tool decisions, and any use that affects how the office produces its work.

Default posture: when in doubt, disclose upward and to anyone affected. Non-disclosure erodes trust faster than the AI use itself.

Module 2 · Making disclosure verifiable

Documenting AI use is the literacy skill that turns disclosure into an audit trail.

Saying “I used AI” is not enough. Say it in a way another person could reconstruct.

Time accessed

Date and time. Models change frequently; yesterday’s answer may not be today’s.

Model and version

Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro. Not just “ChatGPT.” The specific model is what makes the record useful.

The prompt

What you actually asked, verbatim. Paraphrasing loses the reasoning that produced the output.

Context provided

Files attached, prior turns, system instructions, or reference material you gave the model. The output depends on all of it.

Without these, “the AI said so” is unfalsifiable. With them, a colleague, an auditor, or a sponsor can rerun the query and see for themselves.

Module 2 · Recognizing when your individual choice becomes a collective problem

The common that AI depletes is trust.

Each AI-drafted proposal, email, or audit response is defensible on its own. Every unverified one contributes to eroding sponsor and institutional trust in the pipeline as a whole.

Individual use is often rational: you save time and the document looks fine. The cost is paid collectively, in the trust the profession needs to keep operating.

Four things compete for the context window.

The window has a fixed size. Understanding that these four categories compete for the same space is what lets you diagnose why an AI seemed to forget what you told it earlier in a long conversation.

Module 4 · Data classification drives tool choice

Which tool for which data?

ChatGPT free tier, Gemini free, Claude free.

  • Fast, accessible, no procurement
  • Inputs may train the model
  • Not for PII, PHI, FERPA, or export-controlled data
Good for: public FOAs, summarizing published papers, drafting boilerplate.

Institutional Copilot, private Claude/Gemini via enterprise agreement, Vandalizer.

  • Inputs stay within your institution's data boundary
  • Approved for defined sensitivity levels (varies by contract)
  • Confirm your BAA / data-processing terms before use
Good for: internal proposal review, sponsor correspondence drafts, budget analysis.

SQL queries, structured spreadsheets, purpose-built scripts.

  • Reproducible. Auditable. Deterministic.
  • Required when regulators need to see the computation
  • Rigid: needs clean structured data going in
Good for: financial audits, effort certification, compliance reporting.

Plain-language check

Contract flow-down

Reads prime terms, drafts the subaward clause set your office typically uses.

Sponsor solicitation check

Compares a proposal against solicitation requirements. Flags gaps before submission.

Progress report assist

Assembles boilerplate sections; investigator focuses on the science and outcomes.

OCR on scanned files

Modern AI reads image-only PDFs and scans well, but this is a classic garbage-in, garbage-out setting. A crooked or low-resolution scan produces wrong dates, wrong dollar figures, and wrong names, all in the same confident tone. Always verify OCR output against the source.

Session outcomes

Learning objectives.

  1. Explain foundational AI concepts and terminology relevant to research administration.
  2. Identify tasks and workflows in research administration where AI and automation can add value.
  3. Select appropriate AI models and break complex tasks into manageable components across different AI tools and platforms.
  4. Design effective and accurate AI prompts for specific administrative tasks.
  5. Evaluate AI outputs for accuracy, reliability, and appropriateness, and apply iterative refinement strategies broadly across AI systems.
  6. Be prepared to discuss and address ethical, transparency, and governance considerations in AI-assisted workflows.
