**Course:** AI and Data Science for Research Administrators

## Module Overview

This module explores how to navigate compliance, risk, and institutional governance when integrating artificial intelligence into research administration workflows. As AI tools rapidly evolve, research administrators must ensure institutional practices adhere to federal, sponsor, state, and internal guidelines. Learners will examine the multi-level regulatory landscape — spanning federal agencies (NSF, NIH, DOE), state legislation, and institutional oversight — and learn how to apply the traditional "Compliance Pyramid" to AI adoption. The module highlights practical risk management strategies for pre- and post-award workflows, data security and privacy classifications (PII, PHI, FERPA, export controls), criteria for selecting appropriate tool tiers (Public vs. Private AI vs. Data Science), and methods for maintaining human oversight, documentation, and audit trails across AI-assisted tasks.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, learners will be able to:

- Apply the Compliance Pyramid framework to evaluate AI usage against institutional, sponsor, state, and federal regulations.
- Evaluate multi-level AI governance structures across federal funding agencies, state laws, and institutional oversight models.
- Embed targeted AI risk management controls, disclosures, and attestations directly into existing pre-award routing, DMPs, and post-award processes.
- Utilize the 5W1H framework to develop or update institutional AI policies and acceptable use guidelines.
- Classify research data types (PII, PHI, FERPA, export-controlled) and determine when to use Public AI, Private AI, or Traditional Data Science methods.
- Establish internal controls, including human-in-the-loop validation and audit trails, to build accountability and transparency into AI-assisted administrative processes.

## 4.0 Basics

### Compliance

Within the world of research administration, compliance is a term that we hear about or deal with daily. It is also an essential consideration of AI use.

When utilizing AI within our tasks and responsibilities as research administrators, we strive to ensure our institution maintains compliance with federal, sponsor, state, and institutional guidelines. It is important to keep these guidelines at the forefront of our AI usage to ensure that they are followed.

Many of you may be familiar with the "Compliance Pyramid." The idea of this pyramid is that the strictest policy/guideline should be followed.

Start at the top and work your way down the pyramid.

For example, if your institution does not have a policy on pre-award costs, refer to the award terms, if there is nothing in the award terms, look at the program requirements, etc.

In other words, you must follow the most restrictive guidelines.

*[Compliance Pyramid figure]*

This same pyramid can be referred to when considering compliance with AI use. The key difference you may notice when attempting to incorporate a similar approach for AI, is that there is still a lot emerging in terms of AI governance. It may be difficult to find the answer you are looking for unless you create your own policies or guiding principles at your institution or even within your own office!

Overall, when considering compliance and the implications of AI, it is up to the individual and institution to ensure compliance is retained and that we are all following the necessary regulations even if we are changing our processes.

### Risk

When considering AI usage and incorporation into our practices, it is important to recognize the potential risks associated with it. Things such as privacy and ethical implications should be discussed and addressed prior to engaging with certain AI use cases. AI Ethics has already been discussed in depth in Module 2; however, this module will relate the risks directly to the context of research administration compliance.

### AI Governance

There is currently no single set of guidelines or principles that widely govern AI. It is up to each individual, institution, state, etc. to determine their own AI governance principles. The following sections will provide you with guidance and considerations that you may choose to use as AI use is addressed at your own institution.

By keeping these viewpoints in mind, you can:

- Promote responsible and ethical use of AI across the research enterprise
- Reduce compliance, legal, and reputational risk
- Ensure consistency with sponsor, institutional, and regulatory expectations
- Decrease AI ethics incidents (e.g., privacy issues)

What we don't want these things to do:

- Create fear
- Overly restrict AI use to where it's not utilized in any circumstance
- Stymie opportunities to grow

## Section 4.1 Governance Frameworks and Risk Management Strategies

### Key Levels of AI governance

The overall trend of AI governance/guidelines is a growing, complex, and sometimes fragmented regulatory environment across the US, where (for now) states are often leading the way in establishing guardrails ahead of comprehensive federal action.

#### Federal

Currently, there is no federal law to govern AI within the United States. While there may be governance guidelines in the future from the U.S. federal government, it is important to start now to create and implement AI governance principles for your own organization. Other governments have implemented AI laws and guidelines that must be followed.

- E.g., European Union AI Act (requirements are specific to the identified risk level). 🔗 <https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/>
- LIVING GUIDELINES ON THE RESPONSIBLE USE OF GENERATIVE AI IN RESEARCH PUBLISHED: 🔗 <https://european-research-area.ec.europa.eu/news/living-guidelines-responsible-use-generative-ai-research-published>

#### Sponsor Specific guidelines

Another federal lens that should be complied with is any sponsor-specific guidelines regarding AI usage. While it is anticipated that regulations may continue to adapt, here are a few examples of federal agencies who have established agency-specific governance models:

- **National Science Foundation (NSF)** has established a two-tiered AI Governance ecosystem comprising three governing bodies: an AI Governance Board (AIGB), supported by an AI and Data Committee (AIDC) and an AI Steering Committee (AISC). Both committees report to the Board. The tiered structure for internal AI governance ensures rigorous oversight, transparency and accountability across the entire AI lifecycle. The AI Governance board will provide strategic oversight over the agency's integration of AI.
  Source: 🔗 <https://www.nsf.gov/policies/ai-policy>

- **National Institutes of Health (NIH)** has a robust system of policies and practices that guide stakeholders across the biomedical and behavioral research ecosystem. While AI may not be explicitly mentioned, NIH's policy framework is designed to responsibly guide and govern advancing science and emerging technologies, including development and use of AI technologies in research.
  Source: 🔗 <https://osp.od.nih.gov/policies/artificial-intelligence/>

- **U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)** strategy focuses on effective risk management and fostering innovation, aiming to build a mature, resilient, and trustworthy AI ecosystem. The Department recognizes that a uniform "one-size-fits-all" governance approach would stifle innovation in some areas while being insufficient in others. Therefore, a differentiated strategy will be adopted, while reinforcing the human and structural foundations.
  Source: 🔗 <https://www.energy.gov/topics/artificial-intelligence#:~:text=Learn%20More-,Responsible%20AI,-AI%20and%20National>

#### State

Despite no U.S. federal law, several states have issued their own AI laws or governance framework. Examples include:

- **Utah** — Established the nation's first dedicated Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and appointed a Chief Data & AI Officer supported by a cross-agency AI Council.
  Source: <https://ai.utah.gov/>
- **Idaho** — Provides Framework for Responsible Innovation. The framework is designed to position the state at the forefront of responsible artificial intelligence innovation in state government.
  Source: 🔗 <https://its.idaho.gov/ai/?utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery>

It is important to note that most, if not all, of the current state governance is broadly addressing use of AI. There is likely limited guidance at the state level that has yet specifically addressed AI use in the lens of research administration & compliance. U.S. State AI Law Tracker — Several websites have trackers for the various AI state laws: 🔗 <https://ai-law-center.orrick.com/us-ai-law-tracker-see-all-states/#:~:text=AI%20in%20Political%20Advertising,%E2%80%A2>

#### Institution

Now that you have a better understanding of the government's approach to AI governance (or in some cases, lack of governance), it is important to consider how your institution is addressing or plans to address AI governance. Institutional Governance of AI is essential, especially when considering the risk and compliance we face in the world of research administration.

**Establishing institutional AI governance structures**

Each institution will be unique in how they have decided to approach AI Governance.

Institutions may consider establishing AI governance structures such as oversight committees (AI task force, compliance council, etc.) to monitor creation and implementation of AI policies and procedures.

While these types of structures may not be led by the Office of Sponsored Programs team, it is valuable to have an OSP team member as part of the structure.

**Cross-Function Collaboration** — These types of structures should incorporate individuals from multiple key areas on campus such as IT, Legal, OSP, Research Compliance, etc. [^kg1]

If efforts are not happening institution-wide, start with sponsored program-specific processes and procedures.

### Risk Management Approaches

**Incorporating AI considerations into existing risk assessment frameworks**

Research administration is deeply tied to risk management. Incorporating AI considerations into existing research administration risk assessment frameworks can often be done by embedding targeted prompts, attestations, or checks into processes that already exist (rather than building entirely new workflows)

**Examples:**

**Add AI Disclosure Questions to Pre-Award Routing Forms** — One of the easiest integration points is the internal proposal routing form. This helps institutions identify AI use before proposal submission.

- Were generative AI tools used in the development of this proposal (e.g., text drafting, literature summaries, data analysis, figures)?
- If yes, please describe how AI was used.
- Did any AI tools process non-public research data, confidential information, or controlled data?
- Were sponsor-specific AI policies reviewed (if applicable)? — Remember, some sponsors may suggest or require disclosure of AI use.

**Incorporate AI Questions into Data Management Plans** — Many federal sponsors (e.g., NIH, NSF, etc.) already require data management or sharing plans, making them a natural place to address AI. Research administrators can prompt investigators to consider:

- Whether AI tools will be used to analyze, classify, or generate research data
- How data privacy and security will be maintained when using AI tools
- Whether AI outputs will be validated or verified

**Add AI Attestations During Proposal Submission** — Institutions can require investigators to attest to responsible AI use as part of proposal certification.

**Add AI Questions to Post-Award Setup** — AI considerations do not stop at proposal submission. During award setup, administrators could ask:

- Will AI tools be used during the project period?
- Will project data be used to train machine learning models?
- Will AI-generated outputs be included in publications or reports?

**Training/Education** — One of the most impactful risk management strategies is ensuring that faculty/staff are properly educated on AI use and governance. Consider creating targeted trainings that address these topics and give individuals the space to ask questions.

## Section 4.2 Institutional Policies and Acceptable Use

As AI tools rapidly enter the research and administrative environments, institutions must set clear boundaries around acceptable, ethical, and compliant use. Research administrators play a crucial role in translating these policies into practice for faculty.

### Developing AI use policies

As an institution navigates the development of an institutional AI policy, it is important to approach the policy with specific lenses in mind. Institutional Governance of AI is essential, especially when considering the risk and compliance we face in the world of research administration. Each institution will be unique in how they have decided to approach AI Governance.

Examples:

- **University of Florida:** 🔗 <https://ai.ufl.edu/research/guidance-for-researchers/#:~:text=Researching%20AI%20and%20Using%20AI,of%20knowledge%20in%20their%20fields.>
- **Harvard University:** 🔗 <https://it.hms.harvard.edu/about/policies-and-guidelines/generative-ai>

Your institution may choose to govern AI broadly (e.g., institution wide policy), but they may also decide there is benefit in putting guidelines in place specific to sponsored programs, research, and compliance. While we don't want to limit ourselves in the ways we can use AI, it is important that we are educating faculty/staff to ensure AI is being used responsibly and compliantly.

### Considerations on Institutional AI governance

**Broad Considerations:**

- The framework referenced in this article describes how to systematically analyze AI governance proposals through six key questions: 🔗 <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S266665962500023X>
- **What** should be regulated (data, algorithms, sectors, or risk levels),
- **Why** regulate (ethics, legal compliance, market failures, or institutional/national interests),
- **Who** should regulate (industry, government, or public stakeholders),
- **When** regulation should occur (upstream, downstream, or lifecycle approaches),
- **Where** it should take place (local, national, or international levels), and
- **How** it should be enacted (hard versus soft regulation).
- Full article citation: S. Matthew Liao, Iskandar Haykel, Katherine Cheung, Taylor Matalon, Navigating the complexities of AI and digital governance: the 5W1H framework, *Journal of Responsible Technology*, Volume 23, 2025, 100127, ISSN 2666-6596, 🔗 <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrt.2025.100127>.
- In a broad AI policy, an institution may choose to address acceptable use of generative AI in research, teaching, and administration in a sole policy.

**Institutional Sponsored Projects Specific Considerations:**

Example — an institution may establish a policy or process that informs faculty, prior to proposal development, about permissible and prohibited uses of AI tools. ## Section 4.3 Regulatory Touchpoints for AI in Research

This section will help you navigate the complex regulatory and compliance landscape of AI integration by aligning your data management practices with institutional policies, federal privacy laws, and specific sponsor requirements.

### Data Management and Privacy

Ensuring that the way AI tools handle, store, or process research data follows both:

- Your institution's internal rules and policies about data management and protection, and
- The external requirements set by sponsors about how project data should be managed.

Every university or research organization typically has a data governance framework — this is a set of internal policies that define:

- What types of data exist at the institution (e.g., public, internal, restricted, confidential)
- Who owns and can access those data
- How data can be stored, shared, and disposed of
- What technical safeguards (encryption, approved storage systems, etc.) are required
- UI Research Data policy for example: 🔗 FSH 5700 - Research Data | University of Idaho

For example:

> B. Bacon University may have a policy saying research data must be stored on approved university servers (not in personal cloud accounts or unvetted third-party AI tools).

As summarized in the previous unit, it's important to incorporate AI usage into institutional data governance framework.

### Other Considerations of AI/Data Governance on Research Administration Processes

**Confidential or Export-controlled Data** — Data that is confidential or considered export-controlled data should be carefully managed and protected. E.g., Entering confidential or export-controlled data into an AI tool that stores inputs externally could violate institutional data security policies.

**Data Privacy of Research Participants** — Similar to confidential data, sensitive data such as research participant data should also be considered and carefully managed. E.g., Having an AI tool summarize the research findings of a raw data set of collected research information may have the potential to unintentionally share participant data that should have been kept anonymous.

**Proposal Writing using AI tools** — Several sponsors have requirements in regard to using AI in writing proposals.

- **NIH** — Applications "substantially developed by AI" are rejected as non-original. PIs are now limited to 6 applications per year (as of Sept 2025) to curb AI-driven "spamming" of the system.
  Source: 🔗 <https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html>
- **E.g., NSF** — Proposers are "encouraged" to disclose AI use in the Project Description.
  Source: 🔗 <https://www.nsf.gov/news/notice-to-the-research-community-on-ai#footnote4> (Updated specific guidance is expected in the next full version of the NSF PAPPG)

**Other sponsor specific data and AI use requirements** — Each sponsor has unique requirements for data management and sharing that may be exclusive or inclusive of AI. It is recommended to stay up to date on relevant sponsor updates to ensure appropriate compliance.

**Example 1: Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC)**

On November 10, 2025, the Department of Defense (DoD) began the phased implementation of Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC), requiring contracting officers to meet CMMC Level 1 requirements in applicable contracts, thereby safeguarding Federal Contract Information (FCI). Ensuring CMMC compliance becomes more complex when evaluating the requirements against data management systems that integrate artificial intelligence into their processes. AI tools, including large language models such as ChatGPT and Claude, can improve efficiency and innovation but also introduce new risks relevant to CMMC compliance. These risks include reliance on third-party services and limited transparency into model behavior, which creates uncertainty around data handling, data retention, and limits the traceability and auditability of outputs.

Research administrators must therefore pay additional attention to data security practices to ensure FCI is protected in accordance with CMMC requirements when leveraging AI.

See this article for more in-depth information on CMMC compliance with data management and AI usage.

### Data classification and handling

When working with any data, it should be classified to determine if the information is sensitive or protected. A few common definitions and guidelines that should be considered when classifying your data include: [^kg2] [^kg3]

- **Personally Identifiable Information (PII):** Any data that can be used to identify a specific person, either on its own or when combined with other data.
  Examples include names, Social Security numbers, home addresses, and biometric data, but also less sensitive information like dates of birth and ZIP codes, which can be combined to identify an individual
- **Protected Health Information (PHI):** Any individually identifiable health data that can be used to identify a patient. It is protected under the HIPAA Privacy Rule and includes a wide range of information,
  Examples include Medical records, Diagnostic test results, Prescription information, Health insurance information, Demographic data (e.g., name, address, phone number)
- **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA):** A U.S. law that sets national standards for protecting sensitive PHI from being disclosed without consent. Its main purpose is to safeguard patient privacy while also ensuring health insurance portability and improving efficiency and reducing healthcare fraud through standardized electronic transactions.
- **Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA):** A U.S. federal law that protects the privacy of student education records and governs how institutions handle those records.
  For research administrators, FERPA requires ensuring that any access to or use of student data in sponsored projects complies with institutional policies and federal requirements. This includes verifying that researchers have appropriate authorization, that identifiable student information is not disclosed without consent (unless a FERPA exception applies), and that data management plans address confidentiality and security of education records.
- **Proprietary Data:** Information for which the rights of ownership are restricted so that the ability to freely distribute the data is limited.
- **Other Sensitive Data:** Even if the data doesn't fall into one of the above categories, it may still be sensitive. Here are a few questions you should ask/consider before putting data into an AI platform [^kg4] [^kg5]:
  - Could the data be linked (even indirectly) to an identifiable person?
  - Is the information covered under non-disclosure agreements (NDAs), confidentiality clauses, or export control restrictions?
  - Is the data stored or processed outside approved institutional systems?

### Export Controls and Research Security

Export control regulations [^kg6] were not written with modern AI tools in mind — but they still apply when AI systems are used to process, store, train on, or disseminate controlled information.

**Export Administration Regulations (EAR):**

- Govern "dual-use" technologies, software, and technical data
- Apply broadly to many engineering, computing, and data-intensive research areas
- **International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR)** — Govern defense-related technologies and technical data

**AI may become an issue when:**

- Controlled data are entered into open AI tools
- AI systems store or process data on foreign servers
- Outputs generated by AI are shared without export review
- While beyond the general scope of this course, it is also important to note that additional export control issues are created when training AI models using export-controlled datasets

**Research Security Regulations** — AI use intersects with broader federal research security requirements, including:

**NSPM-33 (National Security Presidential Memorandum–33)** Emphasizes:

- Protection of sensitive research data
- Disclosure of foreign affiliations and support
- Institutional risk assessment and mitigation

AI tools can undermine NSPM-33 goals if they:

- Enable undisclosed foreign access to research data
- Obscure data provenance or usage
- Bypass institutional oversight mechanisms

**Agency specific guidelines** — Many federal agencies have issued requirements for research security training for those submitting proposals and engaging in awards.

While in their current form (as of January 2026), none of these training requirements explicitly center on AI content for grant applicants, but research security training increasingly involves topics highly relevant to AI research environments (e.g., cybersecurity, foreign threats to research integrity, and responsible research conduct), which can overlap with AI security concerns.

AI use may be incorporated into future research security training requirements. Be sure to stay up to date with federal sponsor guidelines. As with any grant guidelines, RA's play an essential role in educating PIs on applicable policies and requirements. RA's should stay up to date on relevant policies and guidelines related to export controls, research security, AI use, and overall data management.

## Section 4.4 Building Accountability and Transparency in AI-Assisted Processes

As research administrators, one of our biggest responsibilities is ensuring that institutional processes remain compliant, auditable, and trustworthy. As AI tools become more integrated into research administration workflows, this responsibility extends to how we document, review, and communicate AI-assisted activities.

### Documentation and disclosure practices

- **Recording when and how AI tools are used in administrative or research tasks** — A foundational step in building accountability is recording when and how AI tools are used in administrative or research-related tasks. This does not require excessive or burdensome tracking, but it does mean being intentional.
  For example, if AI is used to draft proposal language, summarize sponsor guidelines, or assist in budget development, that use should be documented in a way that is consistent and retrievable. This could take the form of internal notes, routing form fields, or metadata tags within documents.
- **ER of TaMPER** [^kg7]
- **Transparency in Decision-Making** — Documenting rationale for AI-assisted reviews or analyses — Closely related is transparency in decision-making. When AI tools are used to support reviews or analyses (e.g., risk assessments, compliance checks, or proposal evaluations) it is important to document the rationale behind final decisions.
- Administrators should clearly indicate where AI contributed insights and where human expertise ultimately guided the outcome. This distinction helps prevent overreliance on AI and ensures decisions remain defensible
- **Internal controls for AI-assisted actions:**
  - Review and validation of AI outputs by humans. AI can inform, but it should not replace, professional judgment.
  - A key control mechanism is human review and validation of AI outputs. AI-generated content should never be accepted at face value without verification. Research administrators should critically evaluate outputs for accuracy, completeness, and alignment with institutional and sponsor requirements. This is particularly important in high-stakes areas such as compliance determinations, budget justifications, and regulatory documentation.
- **Audit trails for AI-generated materials**
  - In addition, institutions should consider implementing audit trails for AI-generated materials. This means maintaining a record of how content was generated, including prompts used, versions created, and any subsequent edits. Audit trails do not need to capture every keystroke, but they should provide enough detail to support accountability and reproducibility when necessary.
  - Again, think of the ER of TaMPER — This is one way to make sure AI use is documented in a consistent and trackable way.

### Promoting a culture of ethical AI use [^kg8]

As summarized in Module 2 of this AI Literacy course, ensuring ethical AI use is promoted is an essential aspect of AI implementation. Approaches such as: "Training and awareness programs for staff and faculty" and "Encouraging open discussion of AI limitations and potential misuse" can be a key way to address AI ethics and ensure that it is being maintained as part of the conversation.

## Questions to ask before entering data into an AI tool

*(Section 4.3)*

- Does the data include any personally identifiable information (PII), such as names, addresses, phone numbers, or email addresses?
- Could the data be linked (even indirectly) to an identifiable person?
- Does it include student information protected under FERPA?
- Does it include protected health information (PHI) under HIPAA?
- Are there any data elements that could be re-identified if combined with other public or institutional information?
- Does the dataset contain human subjects data collected under an IRB-approved protocol?
- Has the IRB or sponsor imposed restrictions on data sharing or use?
- Would uploading this data to an external system (like an LLM) violate informed consent terms or data use agreements?
- Is there a certificate of confidentiality or data sharing restriction that limits external use?
- Does the data contain confidential institutional information, such as budgets, internal memos, or review comments?
- Could the data reveal internal business processes, pricing structures, or decision-making workflows?
- Is the information covered under non-disclosure agreements (NDAs), confidentiality clauses, or export control restrictions?
- Would disclosure of this data pose a reputational, financial, or compliance risk to the institution?
- Does the sponsor or funding agreement prohibit sharing or external processing of project data?
- Is the project subject to federal contract clauses, ITAR, or EAR export control restrictions?
- Would uploading content to an LLM violate the sponsor's data security or information handling terms?
- Does the institution classify this type of data as restricted, confidential, or controlled?
- Has institutional leadership (e.g., CIO, OSP, legal) approved the use of LLMs for this data type?
- Is the LLM platform being used a public or non-university-approved system?
- Does the platform store or retain data inputs for model training?
- Is the data stored or processed outside approved institutional systems?
- Could sharing this data with an LLM create compliance, legal, or reputational risk?
- Can the same task be completed using anonymized, de-identified, or synthetic data instead?
