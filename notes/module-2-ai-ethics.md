## 1. What is AI Ethics and Why It Matters

### 1.1 Introduction

AI ethics, in the sense we will use the term in this module, is not a code of conduct. It is the practice of figuring out which questions to ask about an AI tool and how to look for defensible answers that you can defend. The work has two parts, and both matter: knowing which questions to raise, and knowing how to reason through them when reasonable people disagree.

This is harder than it sounds. The hard part is not memorizing a list of principles. It is that the principles often pull in different directions — a tool that is more efficient may also be less transparent; a tool that is more accurate on average may produce worse outcomes for a few relevant tasks; a tool that is good for our office may be bad for the broader research enterprise. Ethical reasoning is the work of holding those tensions while and deciding what to do anyway.

### 1.2 Demo: A Concrete Case [^td4] [^bb5]

Live demo placeholder. Open [^cj6] with a real or recent case from the participants' own work. Good candidates: an office considering whether to route PI communications through an AI drafting tool without disclosure; a sponsor's audit response generated partly by AI; a debate about whether the office should adopt a tool that one staff member objects to on environmental grounds. Let the room name the disagreement before naming the principles.

### 1.3 The Ethical Reasoning Challenge

The familiar phrase — garbage in, garbage out — applies here too, but with a twist. In prompt engineering, the failure mode [^cj7] [^cj8] is producing output that is wrong or unusable. In ethics, the failure mode is producing output that is defensible-sounding but indefensible. You can write a prompt that produces a fluent, professional, internally consistent answer to "is this proposal okay to submit?" and have the answer be exactly the wrong call. Worse: you can convince yourself, after the fact, that the call was reasonable, because you have an articulate, AI-generated rationale to point to.

So ethical reasoning is defensible, but a defensible claim does not necessarily constitute ethical reasioning partly defensive. It is the practice of pressure-testing your own decisions before they produce real consequences and harden into precedent. And like prompt engineering, it is a skill that improves with practice and dialogue.

## 2. Defining the Terms

### 2.1 Descriptive vs. Normative Claims

A useful distinction at the start: To distinguish between a defensible claim that composes ethical reasoning from one that doesn't, it is necessary to recognize a normative claim apart from a descriptive claim. Let's make the distinction: there are two kinds of claims you can make, and they need to be kept apart.

- **Descriptive claims** are about what is the case, what is possible, or what will happen. "Most research administrators in our office now use AI to draft routine emails." That is a claim about the world; you check it by looking.
- **Normative claims** are about what ought to be the case, what is permissible, or what is required. "Research administrators ought to disclose AI use in routine emails." That is a claim about what we should do; you check it by reasoning.

Upholding this distinction is easier said than done. Why this matters. A common move iIn casual conversation, it is common to slide from a descriptive claim straight to a normative one: "Everyone is using AI tools without disclosure, so it must be fine." That move has a name — the naturalistic fallacy — and it does not work. The fact that something is widely done does not, by itself, make it permissible. You cannot derive an ought from a mere is.

That said, there is one connection between the two that does hold. The principle "ought implies can" says that if something ought to be done, it must be possible to do it. When we hold someone morally responsible for a failure, we are implicitly claiming it was possible for them to have acted otherwise. So, if your ethical position requires research administrators to manually verify every AI output line by line, and that is in fact impossible given the volume of work, your position is not actually doing normative work. [^td9]

### 2.2 Individual vs. Collective Action

Another distinction worth drawing early: ethical questions about AI arise at the individual level and the collective two levels, and our interests can conflict as they work between these two levels. they can come apart [^cj10] [^cj11].

- **The individual level:** What should I do, in this situation, given what I know? Should I disclose AI use in this email? Should I run this budget through this tool?
- **The collective level:** What should we — our office, our institution, the research administration profession — do, taken together? Which tools should we adopt? Which norms should we share?

What is ethical for an individual to do may differ from what produces the best collective outcome. An individual administrator might reasonably adopt a particular AI tool; if every office adopts the same tool without coordination, the collective result might be worse than if no one had. Section 7 will return to this in detail. For now, the point is to notice that who is the agent in an ethical question changes the question. [^cj12]

### 2.3 What Normative Inquiry Cannot Do

One last setup point. Unlike the empirical sciences, normative domains like ethics and law cannot rely on observation alone. We always begin from some normative starting points — intuitions, principles, values we hold prior to the inquiry — and refine them through reasoning. The question is how we refine them. That is what the next section is about.

## 4. Why AI Ethics Matters in Research Administration

### 4.1 Demo: Ethics in the Audit Trail

Live demo placeholder. Walk through a short scenario: an institution discovers, during a sponsor audit, that an AI tool was used to produce a key compliance document; the prompt is no longer recoverable; the output is plausibly correct but cannot be reconstructed. Ask the room: who is responsible? what should have been documented? was the use itself wrong, or only the lack of documentation? This sets up the "control and accountability" theme.

Research administration is an unusually high-stakes setting for AI ethics, and the reasons are specific. The work involves federal regulations, sponsor-specific rules, institutional policies, and tight deadlines, often in combination. A poorly considered AI deployment does not just produce a mediocre product. It can produce confidently wrong outputs that propagate through compliance documents, audit responses, or PI communications before anyone notices. So the case for taking ethics seriously is not abstract.

### 4.2 Vulnerability and Trust

Research administrators sit at the center of a network of dependencies. PIs depend on their RA offices you to get budgets right, to read awards correctly, to flag compliance issues before they become incidents. Institutions depend on RA offices you to safeguard data, to maintain sponsor relationships, and to act in ways the institution can stand behind. Sponsors depend on the integrity of the documents you produce. Each of those parties is, in a meaningful sense, made vulnerable by the choices RAs and RA offices you make about AI tools. The first guiding question in Section 7 — who is made vulnerable? — is not rhetorical.

### 4.3 Compliance and Public Trust

Federal sponsors operate on the presumption that the documents they receive reflect the responsible judgment of the institution's research administration staff. Responsibly introducing AI assistance does not constitute a violation of that assumption. AI-assisted documents are not, by themselves, a violation of that presumption. But unconsidered AI use — outputs no one verified [^cj14], prompts no one documented, hallucinated regulatory citations no one caught — is a fast route to the kind of incident that erodes trust well beyond the office that caused it. The reputational cost, not to mention potential legal repercussions, [^td15] of a single high-profile failure is substantial, and it is paid by the entire profession [^cj16].

### 4.4 Fiduciary and Stewardship Obligations

Research administrators are fiduciaries: they steward resources that belong to others (sponsors, institutions, the public). The obligations that come with that role do not pause when an AI tool is in the loop. If anything, the speed and scale at which AI can produce plausible-sounding work raise the stakes. The question, who benefits and who is harmed? is the second guiding question for a reason: AI tools redistribute benefits and harms in ways that are not always visible at the point of adoption.

### 4.5 The Broader Stakes

Beyond the immediate compliance and stewardship picture, AI use in research administration ripples outward. Energy and water consumption are real. Labor markets shift. Norms set in research administration influence norms elsewhere on campus and in the broader sponsor community. None of this means individual administrators are responsible for solving climate change or reshaping labor policy single-handedly. But it does mean the ethical horizon for AI use is wider than the immediate task, and Section 8 will give you tools to think about that wider horizon without becoming paralyzed by it.

## 5. The Cost of Skipping Ethical Reasoning

### 5.1 Pressure-Testing a Plausible Claim

Pick one statement from the workshop instrument you completed in Section 3 where your own answer was confident — Agree or Disagree with no hedging. Now ask yourself a single follow-up: what evidence would change my mind? If you cannot answer that question quickly, your position has not been sufficiently pressure-tested. That is what this section is about.

### 5.2 Common Patterns in Underdeveloped Ethical Reasoning

There are failure modes worth naming in this module, and they will be listed below. A short list of failure modes worth naming. These are the ethical analogs of the "common pitfalls in poor prompts" from Module 3.

- **The unexamined intuition.** "This just feels wrong." The reaction may be tracking something real, but until it is articulated, it cannot be tested or shared.
- **The slide from is to ought.** "Everyone is doing it." See Section 2.2; widespread practice is not an adequate justification.
- **The overgeneralized principle.** "Always disclose AI use." Stated that broadly, the principle has uncomfortable implications (do you disclose use of spell-check?). Either qualify it or watch it collapse under counterexample.
- **The false binary.** "Either we adopt this tool or we fall behind." Real choices almost always have more than two options; the binary framing is rhetorical, not analytical.
- **The deferred responsibility.** "That is IT's problem," or "That is the vendor's problem," or "That is the PI's problem." These statements are Ssometimes true, but they are more. Ooften a way of not engaging.
- **The sunk-principle fallacy.** "We already adopted it, so it must be okay." Past adoption does not warrant is not present justification.
- **The confident hand-wave.** "On balance, the benefits outweigh the harms." Possibly. But this needs to be unpacked: which benefits, to whom, weighed against which harms, to whom? [^cj17]

### 5.3 The Cost of Each

Each of these patterns produces decisions that are defensible-sounding in the moment but brittle under scrutiny. The cost shows up later: at an audit, at a faculty senate meeting, in a sponsor letter, in a press inquiry. By that point, the decision has hardened into precedent and and uprooting precedent is often costly and complicated. the cost of revisiting it is high. Ethical reasoning is the discipline of catching the brittleness early, while the barriers to revisiting the decision remain low. decision is still cheap to revisit [^cj18].

### 5.4 Trust but Verify, Again

The same principle from Module 3 applies. [^cj19] [^cj20] Fast and wrong is more dangerous than slower and right. The discipline is the same: build verification into the process, name the assumptions, write down the reasoning. Ethical reasoning leaves a trail that you can return to when the situation changes. [^cj21]

## 6. Methodologies for Ethical Reasoning

This is the heart of the module [^cj22]. Two methodologies, both philosophical in origin, both well-suited to the kinds of questions research administrators actually face. Neither is a shortcut. Both are practices that get sharpenr with use.

### 6.1 Argument by Analogy

Arguments by analogy are one of the most common tools in ethical reasoning, often used including in everyday office conversations. The basic form is simple:

> In situation X, there is a moral obligation to (or it is not permissible to) do A. Situation Y has the same relevant features as situation X. Therefore, in situation Y there is also a moral obligation to (or it is not permissible to) do A.

A worked example, drawn from the broader AI ethics conversation:

> Introducing a new AI tool is like introducing a new prospective medication. Clinical medicine prioritizes safety, requires regulatory oversight, and has criteria for what is permissible. Therefore, AI technologies in high-stakes settings should also prioritize safety and have regulatory oversight for what is permissible.

The argument moves from a settled case (clinical medicine) to a contested one (AI deployment) by claiming the relevant features carry over. If the analogy holds, the obligation carries over too.

**How to criticize an argument by analogy.** Identify a relevant dissimilarity between X and Y. Not just any difference — a difference that matters for the obligation. "AI tools are not medications because medications go inside the body" is a difference, but it may not be relevant to the question of whether safety oversight is warranted. "AI tools update continuously while medications do not" may be more relevant, because continuous updates undermine the static-evaluation regime that medical regulation depends on. [^cj23] [^cj24]

What counts as relevant is not given by the analogy itself. It is discovered through dialogue. So arguments by analogy are powerful but not self-validating. They are the start of a conversation, not the end of one.

**A research administration example.** "Disclosing AI use in PI communications is like disclosing the use of a paid copyeditor in PI communications. We do not normally disclose copyeditor use. Therefore, we do not need to disclose AI use." The analogy may or may not hold. The relevant question is whether AI drafting tools are relevantly similar to a copyeditor — same level of judgment, same accuracy profile, same accountability — or whether they differ in ways that matter for disclosure.

### 6.2 Reflective Equilibrium

Reflective equilibrium is the second methodology, and it is the workhorse of careful ethical reasoning. It is a way of working toward a consistent ethical position by going back and forth between your gut reactions to specific cases and the broader principles you hold. The idea is that: neither your instincts nor your principles are automatically right. You adjust both until they fit together.

In practice, The practice includes six steps.

1. **Start with a gut reaction.** You encounter a case and you have a strong sense of what is right or wrong. "It feels wrong to let an AI system make hiring decisions without any human review."
2. **Try to articulate why.** What principle is behind that reaction? "Decisions that significantly affect people's lives should involve human judgment."
3. **Test it against other cases.** Does the principle hold? What about a doctor using an AI diagnostic tool — should a human always override it, even when the AI is more accurate? You may feel less sure here.
4. **Adjust.** Maybe the principle needs refining: "Decisions that significantly affect people's lives should involve human judgment, but the role of human judgment may vary depending on the stakes and the reliability of the tool."
5. **Bring in broader ethical considerations.** Does a utilitarian perspective (maximizing overall well-being) push in a different direction than a rights-based perspective (protecting individual autonomy)? Weigh them.
6. **Repeat.** Keep testing your refined principle against new cases. Adjust until your positions feel coherent — not perfect, but consistent and defensible.

The goal is not to arrive at a final answer. It is to develop a position you can articulate and defend, while staying open to revising it when you encounter new cases. Convergence toward a fully shared reflective equilibrium is often presumed to be straightforward when in fact,. Iit is not. The point is the practice, not the destination.

### 6.3 Both Methods Are Iterative and Dialogical

Both methodologies share two features that are easy to miss. They are iterative — you do not work them through once and finish. And they work best in dialogue, whether informal conversation with colleagues or formal publication with a broader community. The reason is the same in both cases: Taking a collaborative approach is necessary for both practices because you cannot test an analogy or a refined principle by yourself. You need someone to push back. The workshop instrument you completed in Section 3 is deliberately structured to provoke exactly that kind of pushback, deliberately. The cohort-results view exists precisely so you have something to push back against.

## 7. Four Guiding Questions

The two methodologies mentioned in Section 6 give you the how of ethical reasoning. Four guiding questions give you the what — the things to look for when you are deciding whether and how to deploy an AI tool [^cj25]. Use them as starting points; they will generate further, more specific questions as you work through them.

### 7.1 Who is Made Vulnerable by the AI Tool?

The first question is about dependencies and exposures.

- **Who is downstream from you** — wWho depends on the work you do? Who is downstream from you? Dependencies may include, but are not limited to PIs, institutional leadership, sponsors, the public.
- **Whom do you depend on?** Who is upstream from you — whom do you depend on? Vendors, IT, training data providers, the people whose data fed the model.

Both directions matter. Downstream parties are exposed to your decisions; upstream parties shape what you can do. Mapping the dependencies is the first move.

### 7.2 How Can Potential Harm Be Minimized?

This question reflects an individual-centric ethical framework: prioritize respecting the agency and well-being of those affected, and take harms to a few as a serious cost, where, and that benefits to the many is not an automatic justification. do not automatically cancel out. [^td26]

- Clinical medicine illustrates the pattern: trials are first done to ensure a prospective treatment is safe. Efficacy comes after.
- The EPA's default model presumes that each increase in exposure to a carcinogen yields increased risk of harm. The harm side of the ledger is taken seriously by default.
- Related concepts: justice, fairness, equity, rights, deontology, care ethics.

For AI in research administration, harm-minimization questions are concrete. Could this tool produce outputs that disadvantage particular PIs, particular fields, particular populations of subrecipients? What guardrails could reduce that risk?

### 7.3 Who Stands to Benefit, and How Can Benefits Be Maximized and Distributed?

This question reflects a utilitarian framework,: emphasizinge aggregate well-being, and accepting that harms to a few can be counterbalanced by benefits to the many — but only if the benefits are real, the harms are honestly counted, and the distribution is examined. Common examples of utilitarian reasoning in practice include cost-benefit analysis in risk management, finance, military operations. [^td27] Examples in research administration could include workload analyses, time-to-submission metrics, and error-rate tracking. [^td28]

Two follow-ups that often matter more than the headline benefit calculation: how do you go about finding out who benefits? and who is excluded from the benefit, and is that exclusion incidental or systematic? [^cj29]

### 7.4 Can the Adoption Be Reversed?

We face moral uncertainty whenever we are unsure which framework to adopt or how to balance competing considerations. A useful strategy under moral uncertainty: favor reversibility. Keep as many options open as possible so the decision can be revisited later.

- **Reversible actions:** writing with a pencil, scheduling meetings, archiving emails.
- **Irreversible actions:** spilling ink on paper, permanent deletion of files, publishing sensitive information.

Most AI adoption decisions sit somewhere in between, and that is exactly where the question is most useful. The follow-ups:

- What plans are in place to detect issues that signal it is time to change course?
- What procedures allow for intervention if needed?
- Do the AI tools we adopt actually permit interventions to be possible? (This is a key question in AI safety and worth raising at the procurement stage, not after.)

A small principle that follows: wWhen in doubt, prefer the reversible option, even if it is slightly less efficient. The optionality is part of the value.

## 8. From Individual Choices to Collective Outcomes

So far, we have treated ethical reasoning as a skill an individual administrator brings to bear on a particular decision. That framing is correct as far as it goes [^cj30], but it is incomplete. A great mMany AI ethics questions in research administration look different — and harder — once you notice that the structure [^cj31] of the situation, not the intentions of the people in it, is doing much of the work.

This is where collective action problems come in [^cj32]. Three of them recur often enough to be worth knowing by name.

### 8.1 The Tragedy of the Commons

**The structure.** A shared resource is open to everyone. Each person benefits by using more of it. But if everyone does, the resource is depleted and everyone loses.

**The classic example.** A group of herders shares a common pasture. Each herder benefits from adding one more animal. But if every herder does this, the pasture is overgrazed and collapses.

**The AI connection.** Compute-intensive AI models consume shared resources like energy and water. Any one organization benefits from training larger or running more queries. If everyone races to scale without constraint, the cumulative environmental cost is borne by all. The same logic applies to data: scraping freely available content benefits individual model-builders, but unchecked extraction can degrade the commons of publicly shared knowledge.

**The research administration connection.** An office benefits from running more tasks through AI, faster. Multiply that across institutions and the environmental footprint. becomes a real entry on the ledger — not because any one office is at fault, but because the structure rewards each office for doing what, in aggregate, harms all. [^td33] [^cj34]

### 8.2 The Prisoner's Dilemma

**The structure.** Two parties would both be better off cooperating. But each has an individual incentive to defect — and if both defect, both end up worse off than if they had cooperated.

**The classic example.** Two suspects are interrogated separately. If both stay silent, each gets a light sentence. If one betrays the other while the other stays silent, the betrayer goes free. If both betray, both get moderate sentences. The rational individual move is to betray, though. Mmutual betrayal is worse for both than mutual cooperation.

**The research administration connection.** Two institutions might both benefit from adopting shared, transparent AI-use policies. But each faces a temptation to quietly adopt tools with fewer guardrails to gain a competitive edge. If both defect, neither has meaningful standards, trust erodes, and everyone — students, faculty, sponsors, the public — is worse off. Sponsor-level coordination, professional-society norms, and mutual transparency are the kinds of structures that make cooperation rational rather than naive.

### 8.3 The Stag Hunt

**The structure.** Cooperation produces the best outcome for everyone, but only if everyone commits. If you are not sure others will cooperate, the safer choice is to dogo it alone — even though that leads to a worse collective result.

**The classic example.** A group of hunters can work together to catch a stag (big payoff for all), but only if everyone participates. Any individual can break off to catch a rabbit (small but guaranteed payoff). If even one person breaks off, the stag hunt fails. The dilemma is not about temptation to cheat. It is about trust. You would prefer to cooperate, but only if you are confident others will too.

**The research administration connection.** A department might agree that everyone will use AI tools transparently and develop shared norms around attribution, disclosure, and quality. This works well if everyone commits. If you suspect colleagues will not follow through, the safer move is to do your own thing [^cj35] — and the shared norms never take hold. The stag hunt highlights why visible commitment, public norm-setting, and trust-building matter for AI governance. The problem is not bad faith; it is the absence of grounds for confidence that cooperation will be reciprocated.

### 8.4 Why Naming the Structure Helps

Recognizing which structure(s) you are in clarifies what kind of intervention is needed. A tragedy of the commons calls for some form of regulation, quota, or shared limit. A prisoner's dilemma calls for binding agreements, transparency mechanisms, or repeated interaction that makes cooperation the rational move. A stag hunt calls for trust-building and visible commitment so that others can rely on you to hold up your end.

Most AI ethics conversations in research administration get stuck because they are framed as individual moral failings when they are really structural problems. It is true that RA professionals People should disclose AI use is true but this notion proves insufficient if the underlying situation is a stag hunt. The question becomes how to build the conditions under which everyone can disclose without being penalized for being the first to do so.

This reframing matters because it changes who needs to act. Individual ethical effort is part of the answer. Structural design — by departments, institutions, professional societies, sponsors — is the rest of it.

## 9. The Reflective Equilibrium Cycle

Ethical reasoning is iterative, in roughly the same way prompt engineering is. Even good ethical positions rarely emerge fully formed on the first pass, and the goal is not to arrive at the perfect view up front. The goal is to converge on something defensible through several rounds of dialogue.

**Infographic placeholder.** The Reflective Equilibrium Cycle: Notice → Articulate → Test → Refine → Hold (provisionally) → Notice again. Show as a loop, not a line.

The basic cycle has five moves.

1. **Notice.** Pay attention when something feels off about an AI deployment, an output, or a decision. The discomfort is data.
2. **Articulate.** Try to put the principle behind your reaction into words. If you cannot, the discomfort cannot be tested or shared.
3. **Test.** Apply the principle to a few other cases. Look for cases where it gives an answer you would reject. Those are the most informative.
4. **Refine.** Adjust the principle, narrow its scope, or identify the conditions under which it applies. Sometimes adjust the original gut reaction instead. [^cj36]
5. **Hold provisionally and re-engage.** Treat your current position as defensible-for-now, not as final. New cases will surface, and the cycle starts again.

A worked-out ethical position is a working document, not a fixed artifact. The same is true at the office level: institutional AI policies should be written so they can be revisited as the technology and the practice evolve.

## 10. Practices for Ongoing Ethical Engagement

Three practices are particularly useful for keeping the work of Sections 6–9 alive in an office, rather than letting it harden into a one-time training exercise.

### 10.1 Re-Run the Workshop Instrument Periodically

The workshop instrument from Section 3 is not a one-shot diagnostic. The statements are deliberately strong — always, must, never — so that views shift visibly as tools change, as norms develop, and as the office gains experience. Used periodically (quarterly, at office retreats, after a major tool adoption), the instrument surfaces where the actual disagreement in the office is, now, not where it was a year ago.

The trick is to treat the cohort distribution as conversation fuel, not as a measurement. The goal is not to compute an average. The goal is to make the disagreement visible — and then work through it using the methodologies from Section 6 and the guiding questions from Section 7.

### 10.2 Ground the Environmental Conversation in Real Data

The environmental footprint of AI is a topic where ethical reasoning often gets bogged down in either dismissal (it cannot be that bad) or paralysis (we should not be using these tools at all). Both stances are easier to hold in the absence of data. Three resources are worth pointing learners at.

- **what-uses-more.com.** Compares the energy and water costs of everyday digital activities using consistent units (Wh per hour, mL per query). Created by Jon Ippolito, Professor of New Media, University of Maine. Also available as a custom ChatGPT GPT.
- **your-digital-life.org.** Helps users explore the environmental footprint of their own digital habits and see how AI fits into their broader digital life. Created by Joel Gladd.
- **ai.uidaho.edu/gpu_telemetry_analysis.html.** Analyzes the environmental footprint of the University of Idaho's GPU cluster using real telemetry data — annual emissions equivalent to about nine US households, with location on Idaho's hydroelectric grid cutting carbon intensity to roughly a third of the national average.

The three resources approach the same question from different angles — comparative energy costs, personal digital habits, and institutional infrastructure. Together they make the conversation harder to short-circuit in either direction.

### 10.3 Make Dialogue a Habit, Not an Event

Both core methodologies — argument by analogy and reflective equilibrium — work best in dialogue. That is not an aspirational claim; it is a practical one. Build dialogue into the office workflow: short standing items on staff agendas, periodic case discussions, written-up summaries when an ethically significant decision was made and what the reasoning was. The goal is to leave a trail. A future colleague, or a future version of yourselves, will need it.

A short verification list, parallel to the one in Module 3, for the kind of ethical reasoning you should expect to be able to point to:

- The specific stakeholders who were considered (the who is vulnerable answer).
- The principle being applied, stated clearly enough to be tested against other cases.
- The reversibility of the decision, and what would trigger a revisit.
- The structural situation (commons, prisoner's dilemma, stag hunt, or none of the above) and what that implies for the appropriate response.
- Anything that would be embarrassing or compliance-relevant if the reasoning later turned out to be wrong.

Is your second pass through the instrument any different than [^td37] your first-pass answers? The places where you shifted, and the places where you did not, are both worth understanding.

### 11.2 Exercise 2: Find the Dissimilarity

Below is an analogy that has come up in real research administration discussions. Identify a relevant dissimilarity — a difference between the two situations that matters for the ethical conclusion — and explain why it matters. There is no single right answer; the exercise is to do the work.

> Using AI to draft a routine response email is like using a form letter library. We have used form letter libraries for decades without disclosing them. Therefore, AI use in routine emails does not require disclosure.

If you cannot find a relevant dissimilarity, that itself is a finding: the analogy may hold, and disclosure may not be required. Be ready to defend whichever side you land on.

## 12. Where to Go from Here

A short recap, in the spirit of taking stock. Two methodologies (argument by analogy, reflective equilibrium) give you ways to work through hard cases. Four guiding questions (vulnerability, harm, benefit, reversibility) give you things to look for. Three structural patterns (commons, prisoner's dilemma, stag hunt) help you see when the ethical problem is bigger than the individual decision in front of you. None of this gives you a final answer. All of it gives you a better answer than not asking the question at all. [^td38] [^bb39]

The work of research administration has always been about responsibility under conditions of incomplete information. AI does not change that. It raises the stakes, accelerates the timeline, and adds a few new categories of mistake to the list. The skills you already have — careful reading, attention to compliance, fluency with regulatory frameworks, judgment about whom to trust with what — transfer almost directly. This module aims to make the transfer explicit.

The next module will take up institutional and policy design: how offices, departments, and institutions can build the environments under which the practices in this module become routine rather than exceptional.

## Appendix A: Quiz

> **Course-builder note.** These eight questions check comprehension of the module's core concepts — the descriptive/normative distinction (Section 2), the failure modes in Section 5, the two methodologies in Section 6, the four guiding questions in Section 7, and the collective action structures in Section 8. Each has a single best answer. They are written for objective assessment and can be lifted into the LMS quiz tool as either a graded quiz or an ungraded self-check. An answer key with brief rationales follows the questions; separate it from the learner-facing version if the quiz is graded.

**1.** A colleague says, "Nearly everyone in our office already uses AI to draft routine emails without disclosing it, so disclosure clearly isn't required." What is the reasoning error?

- A. It violates the principle that "ought implies can," since disclosing every single use of AI is not realistically possible.
- B. It slides from what is widely done to what is therefore permissible — the move known as the naturalistic fallacy.
- C. It mistakes a collective-level question about shared office norms for an individual-level question about one person.
- D. It sets up a false binary between disclosing every use of AI and disclosing none of it whatsoever.

**2.** An office policy requires administrators to manually verify every line of every AI-generated output. Given the actual volume of work, doing so is impossible. According to the module, what is wrong with this position?

- A. It commits the naturalistic fallacy by deriving what the office ought to do straight from what it currently does.
- B. It confuses a descriptive claim about workload with a normative claim about professional responsibility.
- C. It sets up a false binary between verifying every line of output by hand and verifying nothing at all.
- D. Because "ought implies can," a duty that genuinely cannot be fulfilled does no real normative work.

**3.** "We already adopted this tool last year, so it must be fine." Which failure mode in ethical reasoning does this illustrate?

- A. The confident hand-wave.
- B. The sunk-principle fallacy.
- C. The overgeneralized principle.
- D. The deferred responsibility.

**4.** Someone argues, "Introducing a new AI tool is like introducing a new prescription medication, so AI in high-stakes settings should face safety testing and regulatory oversight." What is the module's recommended way to challenge an argument by analogy?

- A. Point out that the two cases are not identical in every respect, since medications and software obviously differ.
- B. Show that the argument's conclusion is normative whereas its premises about medicine are merely descriptive.
- C. Identify a relevant dissimilarity — a difference between the cases that actually bears on the obligation at issue.
- D. Reject it on the ground that an argument by analogy can never establish a genuine moral conclusion.

**5.** Which statement best captures what reflective equilibrium asks you to do?

- A. Move back and forth between your reactions to particular cases and the principles you hold, adjusting either until they fit.
- B. Apply a fixed, predetermined hierarchy of principles consistently to every new case you meet, without ever revising it.
- C. Defer to whatever position the cohort distribution turns out to show is the majority view in the room.
- D. Gather enough empirical evidence about the cases that the ethically correct answer simply becomes observable.

**6.** The four guiding questions each foreground a different ethical consideration. Which pairing is correct?

- A. "Who is made vulnerable?" is essentially a utilitarian question about aggregating benefits across everyone affected.
- B. "Who stands to benefit?" is fundamentally a question about the reversibility of the decision under uncertainty.
- C. "Can the adoption be reversed?" is really just a restatement of the naturalistic fallacy in slightly different words.
- D. "How can harm be minimized?" reflects an individual-centric framework that treats harm to a few as a serious cost.

**7.** Several offices would all prefer to adopt shared, transparent AI-use norms, and none is tempted to cut corners for advantage — but each hesitates because it is not confident the others will follow through, so each defaults to going it alone. Which structure is this?

- A. Tragedy of the commons.
- B. Prisoner's dilemma.
- C. Stag hunt.
- D. Naturalistic fallacy.

**8.** Naming the collective action structure matters because it points to the kind of intervention that fits. Which match is correct?

- A. A tragedy of the commons is addressed mainly by trust-building and the kind of visible commitment that others can actually rely on.
- B. A stag hunt is addressed mainly by imposing a quota, a regulation, or a shared cap on how much each party may use.
- C. A prisoner's dilemma is addressed by binding agreements, transparency, or repeated interaction making cooperation rational.
- D. All three call for the same response: urging the individuals involved to set better intentions and behave more ethically.

### Answer Key and Rationales

1. **B.** The inference from "everyone does it" to "it must be permissible" derives an ought from a mere is — the naturalistic fallacy. Widespread practice is not, by itself, a justification (Section 2.1; see also "the slide from is to ought" in Section 5.2).
2. **D.** "Ought implies can": if a requirement cannot possibly be met, it is not doing genuine normative work — "it is just venting." The position has to be rebuilt around what is actually achievable (Section 2.1).
3. **B.** The sunk-principle fallacy — "We already adopted it, so it must be okay." Past adoption is not present justification (Section 5.2).
4. **C.** You criticize an argument by analogy by finding a relevant dissimilarity — not just any difference, but one that bears on the obligation in question. Option A is the trap: mere non-identity is not enough (Section 6.1).
5. **A.** Reflective equilibrium is the back-and-forth between gut reactions to cases and the broader principles you hold, adjusting both until they cohere — neither instincts nor principles are automatically right (Section 6.2). Option C misreads the cohort view, which is conversation fuel rather than an authority (Section 10.1); option D ignores that normative inquiry cannot rest on observation alone (Section 2.3).
6. **D.** Harm-minimization reflects an individual-centric framework that treats harms to a few as a serious cost that benefits to the many do not automatically cancel out (Section 7.2). Benefit-maximization is the utilitarian question (7.3), and reversibility is the response to moral uncertainty (7.4); the other options scramble these pairings.
7. **C.** The stag hunt's signature is that the obstacle is trust, not temptation: everyone would prefer to cooperate but will only do so if confident others will too. In a prisoner's dilemma, by contrast, each party has a standing incentive to defect (Section 8.3).
8. **C.** A prisoner's dilemma calls for binding agreements, transparency, or repeated interaction; a tragedy of the commons calls for regulation, quota, or a shared limit; a stag hunt calls for trust-building and visible commitment (Section 8.4). Option D is the framing the module explicitly warns against — treating structural problems as individual moral failings (Section 8.4).
